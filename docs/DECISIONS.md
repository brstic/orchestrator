# Decisions

Newest first. Each entry: what was decided, why, and what was rejected. Add one whenever a later reader would otherwise have to guess.

## 2026-11-11 — A phone is turned away at the door
- **Refused by the server, before anything is looked at** (`apps/server/src/phone.ts`, one `onRequest` hook): a phone asking for a page gets a page that says the office is for a computer; asking for anything else it gets a 403 that says the same. Before the routes, so an invitation code opened on a phone is not exchanged — it still works on a computer. The page itself checks too (`main.tsx`), for a phone whose browser says it is a desktop one.
- **Told by the User-Agent ("Mobi", iPhone, …), which is a courtesy and not a lock.** Who may enter is decided by invitations and tokens; this only keeps people out of something that cannot work on their device. Anyone can make a browser claim otherwise, and that is fine.
- **Phones only.** A tablet has the screen for it and is let in (an iPad says it is a Mac anyway). Machines are never judged by this: host agents (`/ws/agent`), employees' sessions (`/mcp`) and health checks pass.
- No switch for it: the owner asked for phones to be refused, and nothing in the office is built for one.

## 2026-11-11 — How other people's characters are moved
- **Drawn in the past, between two reports — not chasing the newest.** `RemoteTrack` (`packages/office-3d/src/remote-motion.ts`) keeps the last reports of a person and the character is drawn 300 ms behind, interpolated between the two reports around that moment. Chasing the newest report with an easing made the speed surge and sag ten times a second, and the gait — which walks by how the body really moves — stumbled with it. 300 ms because a window reports every 100 ms and the office relays every 100 ms: two reports that follow each other can arrive nearly 300 ms apart.
- **By the sender's clock.** Each report carries the window's own time (`presence.at`); only the *difference* between its clock and ours is estimated (the quickest trip seen), and it is followed gently (a tenth of real time at most) so a correction never shows as a jump. Without it — an older window — arrival times are used and the walk is visibly less even. No clock synchronisation and no server timestamps are needed.
- **Never ahead of what was reported.** When the reports stop, the character waits where it last was. Guessing ahead looked smoother until the guess was wrong and the character stepped back.
- **"Moving" means going somewhere.** A window used to say "moving" while its person only looked around; the others then saw walking on the spot. Turning is still reported, as a turn. A report that says "not moving" also marks where somebody stood — the walk that follows starts from there, one report's time before the next one, instead of a slow drift across the pause.
- **The position is set in the frame loop only, and before the character animates** (`useFrame(…, -1)`, as employees' characters do). A position in the JSX was re-applied at every report — the jump described above — and a mover that ran after the character made the legs a frame late.
- **A report redraws nobody.** The places go into a map the frame loop reads; React redraws a person only when something about how they look changes (what they say, a gesture, the whip).

## 2026-11-11 — Stepping into a task in progress
The owner: "I need to be able to stop somebody's task, or correct it".
- **Two acts, both of which reach the session.** *Correct* (`TaskManager.correctTask`): the task's text and/or a note; the task stays theirs and in progress. *Stop* (`stopTask`): the task leaves their hands — on hold (BACKLOG, unreleased, assignee and branch kept) or deleted. Moving a card on the board (`PATCH status`) stays what it was, a change to the record; it never told anybody anything, which is why it was not enough.
- **Interrupting is a real Escape, and it is the default for a correction.** Someone going the wrong way should stop going that way now; a note that waits for the end of a long turn arrives after the damage. The box can be unticked for "when you get to it".
- **A stopped task's half-done work is committed by the office on the task's own branch** (`git.wip` on the machine: `add -A`, one commit marked WIP, hooks not run). Without it the employee's next task could not start — a branch is never switched over uncommitted files — and "stop" would leave them stuck, which is what the test found. Only in a worktree of the employee's own and only on a branch the office made (`orch/…`); in a folder worked in place nothing is touched. Rejected: `git stash` (invisible, easily lost, and not on the branch where the task continues); discarding (it is somebody's work); asking the employee to tidy up first (a race with the scheduler; kept only as the fallback when the machine's agent is too old for `git.wip`).
- **On hold, not "ready and unassigned".** A stopped task that went straight back to READY would be handed out again within seconds — to the same employee, by the scheduler. Releasing it again is the owner's word.
- Not built: stopping from the board card or the employee's menu (the task's window is one click from both); an employee stopping a colleague's task (they can already block, hand off and ask).

## 2026-11-11 — What a look at the live office turned up: prompts in the terminal, refused continuations, restarts
The owner asked for a look at what goes on on the server and for whatever stands in the office's way to be fixed. Three things were found in the records and one in the log; each is fixed with a test that fails without the fix.

- **A permission prompt that falls back to the terminal stays the office's business.** The hook can only be held for a while and not across a restart; after that the dialog is in the terminal and the hook is gone. Until now that ended the office's part (a notice in one case, nothing in the other). Now the session's screen — which every machine already sends, and sends afresh when it reconnects — is read for Claude Code's dialog (`permissionPrompt`), and a prompt nobody is asking about becomes an ordinary approval request on the `terminal` channel. Rejected: holding the hook for ever (it cannot outlive the office's process, and Claude Code has its own limit); re-running the tool call (the office cannot, and should not); typing the answer blind (the keys are worked out from the options on screen at the moment of answering, and nothing is pressed when the prompt is no longer the last thing there).
- **The office presses *Yes* or *No* only on a person's decision, and never the widening option.** The request is of kind `permission`: nobody but an owner (or whoever may work with that employee) can decide it, the foreman is refused as always, and nothing is auto-approved — the high-risk rule is exactly what put that prompt there. "Yes, and don't ask again …" is never chosen: it would change what the session may do from then on, which is a different decision.
- **"No conversation found" is a refusal, whatever else was heard.** The office told a failed continuation by "no sign of life since the start". Claude Code does give one — it reports the end of the attempt through a hook — so the test never fired and the same start was repeated. Its own words now count too (only when the session had no turn since that start).
- **The launcher waits for its children; the database waits for a lock from the first statement.** Both are about a restart meeting the previous process. The "database is locked" seen live could not be reproduced exactly (the ordering bug at opening could, and is tested); with the launcher waiting, the two processes should no longer overlap at all. To watch at the next deploys: no `database is locked`, no `(node) remains running after unit stopped`.
- **Left as they are, on purpose:** a session's state after a restart is "unknown" until its next signal (the office does not guess); a message typed into a terminal and never acknowledged stays "sent" (it will not claim a confirmation it did not get); work does not cross between the shared office and a member's own computer for *waiting* (a message does cross).

## 2026-11-11 — Files on tasks; a browser for employees
- **"Vision" needed no model work: Claude Code's Read tool already shows an image to the model.** What was missing was the image reaching the session and the employee being told to look. So the feature is transport and instructions, not a new capability. Rejected: describing pictures in text on the server (a second model call, and a description is worse than the picture); pasting images into the terminal (works only for a person at a keyboard).
- **The office keeps the bytes; the task keeps the list; the machine gets a copy.** Files live in the office's data folder beside the database (not in it: a 25 MB blob per row serves nobody), `Task.attachments` is metadata, and a copy is put in the host agent's own folder on the employee's machine — outside every repository, so it is never committed, never dirties a tree, and a worktree that is thrown away does not take it along. Sessions are started with `--add-dir` for that folder.
- **In pieces, both ways.** People's uploads go in pieces under half a megabyte (a proxy in front of the office usually refuses more than 1 MB in one request; ours does), and the office and a machine exchange 1 MB pieces over the link they already have (8 MB a message). No second channel, no public URL, nothing a machine has to open a port for.
- **Evidence is pulled by the office, not pushed by the employee.** `attach_file` names a path; the machine's agent reads it — only from its allowed roots, its own folders and the temp folder, and never one of the office's own secret-bearing files there. Rejected: letting the model send base64 through a tool argument (it would pass megabytes through the model's context, and models truncate).
- **Only plain picture formats are ever shown; everything else is a download.** An SVG can carry script and an HTML file is a page: served from the office's own address they would run as the office. So `GET …/attachments/:id` sends PNG/JPEG/GIF/WebP as themselves and anything else as `application/octet-stream` with `nosniff` and a sandboxing policy, and the window fetches with the person's token and shows a blob.
- **The browser is Playwright's MCP server, installed per machine, never fetched by the office.** It is the maintained tool for exactly this (structured page snapshots instead of pixels, screenshots when asked), it runs headless on a server, and it speaks MCP, so a session gets it as one more entry in the tool config it is started with. The office only *finds* it (`findBrowserMcp`) — installing software on a machine stays a person's act, written down in `ATTACHMENTS.md`. Pinned version, the machine's own Chrome where there is one, `--isolated` so that no login or cookie outlives a session. Rejected: a browser run by the office and shared by all employees (one session's logins would be another's); screenshots by a shell command (no page, no clicking); Claude in Chrome (it drives a person's own browser, on a person's computer).
- **On by default where installed, one switch for the office.** The owner asked for it for everyone; a machine without the tool simply has none. The risk that comes with it — a page can say anything, and what a page says can steer a model — is the same one a session already runs with `curl`; it is said in the setting's own text and in `SECURITY.md`.

## 2026-11-11 — A shared folder is worked in side by side; a session that lost the office is started again
The owner: "agents must be able to work in the same folder without waiting for each other — we wait for /Projects/xagio although that is only the root, and they mostly work in subfolders", and "see why *Xagio on Branko* could not finish its work and fix everything that is in its way".

- **This replaces "A shared folder is a queue" (below) as the default.** The queue removed the red marks but kept the waiting, and on the live office the waiting was for nothing: the project's folder is a plain folder with nine repositories in it, the employees each have their own, and every task still ran at the root because a task's folder was always the project's.
- **An employee's own folder inside the project's folder is where their tasks run** (`TaskManager.repoFor`). That alone removes the case the owner hit. A folder outside the project's is ignored as before (the project decides), and so is one on another machine.
- **In the very same folder (or the same repository), side by side — and the bookkeeping gives way, not the people.** What used to justify "one at a time" was the office's own completion check (every new uncommitted file blocks) and git's index. So: once a folder is shared, a task's uncommitted work is the files its own session wrote (Write/Edit hooks → `Task.touched`); everything else is left as it is and written in the task's history. Both sides are told who is there and the three rules (stage by path, touch nothing you did not change, wait on `index.lock`). Rejected: a lock per file (the model cannot be held to it, and a hook that denies writes would stall work on false alarms); a worktree per employee forced onto in-place projects (the owner chose in-place because the folder *is* the deployment).
- **Known gaps, accepted:** a file changed only through a shell command (`sed -i`, a generator) is not in `touched`, so in a shared folder it does not block its author's completion; the commits listed as a task's evidence are all commits since it started, a colleague's included (they commit as the same git user); a verification command runs against the folder as it is, with the colleague's half-done work in it. Where these matter, the project's switch **One at a time in a shared folder** (`policy.oneAtATime`) brings the queue back, or the project uses worktrees.
- **A session whose Claude Code says the office's tools are "not connected" is started again by the office** (`SessionRegistry.reconnect`), with the same conversation, when its turn ends — and is not nudged meanwhile. The employee in question had done the work and could not say so: every `complete_task`, `ask_boss` and `report_blocker` failed, the office nudged it three times to "call complete_task", and then everyone waited. The address and token a session uses for the office are given to it at start, so a new start is the cure whatever the cause. Twice an hour at most; then the owner is told, because then something on that machine needs a person.
- **The cause in this case, fixed at its root: the relay on a member's computer now comes back on the port it had** (`startRelay(…, preferredPort)`, remembered in the office's folder on that computer). It used to take any free port at every start of the app, and a running session keeps the address it was started with — so every restart of the desktop app cut every session on that computer off from the office for good. Rejected: rewriting the session's `mcp.json` (Claude Code has read it already); a fixed port for everyone (two offices on one computer, or anything else on that port).

## 2026-11-11 — Several Claude accounts; deleting an employee
- **An account stays what it was: a Claude config folder on a machine.** The office already ran employees with `CLAUDE_CONFIG_DIR`; "several accounts" is a list of such folders per machine (`Host.claudeAccounts`) plus a rule for moving people. Rejected: keeping tokens or API keys in the office and handing them to sessions (the office would hold what it has never held, and a subscription login is not a token one may copy around); one tmux server or one OS user per account (nothing needs it).
- **The owner logs in; the office only makes the folder and shows the command.** Logging in is a browser flow between a person and Claude Code. The office reads two fields of `.claude.json` to say who is logged in, as before. Rejected: a terminal in the office window for the login (an unauthenticated-looking remote terminal for something done once per account; `ssh` is already there).
- **Two signals for "used up": the status line's percentages and a refused turn.** The percentages are exact but do not cover every limit and arrive only at the end of a turn; `StopFailure` covers the rest. A refused turn marks the account for an hour (or until a known reset) because nothing says when that limit ends.
- **Moved, never moved back.** Moving back at a reset would restart sessions twice for nothing and ping-pong between two nearly-full accounts. Ten minutes between two moves of one employee for the same reason.
- **The conversation follows through a shared `projects` folder (a link), not by copying transcripts.** One place for conversations means `--resume` works on any account and nothing has to be kept in step. If Claude Code will not continue it, the existing "could not be continued → new conversation" path applies. Not tried with real Claude Code — written down as such in `ACCOUNTS.md`.
- **Same machine only.** Moving an employee to another machine means moving their folder and their conversation; that is a different feature.
- **Deleting an employee is a second act beside archiving, not a replacement.** Archive keeps everything and can be undone; delete removes the employee and what is theirs and cannot. The tasks are a choice in the dialog (ticked by default, as the owner asked for "everything they have"): with them, or back on the board. The feed, the audit log, meetings and the long memory keep what happened; nothing on disk is touched — the same line `deleteProject` draws. Like a project, an owner's act on what is shared, a member's on their own computer.

## 2026-11-11 — `check:changed`, the first idea taken from T3 Code
- **A `check:changed` script of our own, not T3 Code's.** The idea is theirs (`docs/plans/IDEAS-from-t3code.md`, item 4); the code is written for our layout. `scripts/check-changed.mjs`, run as `pnpm check:changed`.
- **Scope is "the tests beside what changed": a changed file's workspace (`packages/<x>` / `apps/<x>`), and its `test/` folder.** It does not walk the dependency graph to the packages that depend on what changed — that is what `pnpm test:all` is for before shipping, and the script says so when it passes. Rejected: building the dependent-package graph (more to get wrong, and the full suite already covers it); a replacement for the full suites (a change to a base package like `contracts` can break a dependent this never runs).
- **No lint step.** T3 Code's does lint, typecheck and tests; this repo has no linter, so there is nothing to lint. Typecheck is the whole `tsc -p tsconfig.json` (TypeScript's project graph cannot type a subset reliably), skipped when no typechecked `.ts` changed.
- **Working tree by default, `--base <ref>` for a branch.** The everyday case is the edit in hand (staged, unstaged, untracked); `--base` adds everything committed since a ref's merge-base, for checking a whole branch at once. End-to-end tests are off by default (whole-system, slow) and only the changed `tests/e2e` files run.
- **A "hit every surface" checklist in `CLAUDE.md`** (roles, shared vs personal, local vs server, desktop vs browser, both runtimes; a bug fix starts with a failing test) — the surfaces this office has that are easy to forget. Also from the T3 Code reading (item 4).

## 2026-11-11 — Waiting for a colleague
- **`wait: true` on the two request tools, and one new tool, `wait_for_colleague`.** A model already knows `request_collaboration`; whether it can go on without the answer is one more fact about the same request, so it is one more argument. The new tool does what no existing one could: wait for a task, and pick a wait up again. Rejected: a separate "ask and wait" tool (two tools that send the same request, chosen before the model has read either); stretching `wait_for_answer` over colleagues and tasks (it is about the boss, and the end of a task is not an "answer"); `wait` on `send_message` (nothing ties a later message to it as its answer).
- **The call returns when the colleague answers, not when the work is finished.** An answer usually carries what was needed (a contract, a fact). When it is "part of my task" or "a separate task", the result names that task and says how to wait for it. Rejected: holding on by itself until the task is done — minutes become hours, and the one who asked could not use the contract meanwhile.
- **Four minutes per call (`timings.colleagueWaitMs`), like `ask_boss`.** It is the only length a held MCP call has been run with, and the client's time limits are raised for both. After it the call says "not yet", names the continuation, and the wait *stands*: it stays on the session's record and whatever comes later is delivered as a message — the colleague's answer as before, the end of a task as a message from the office.
- **What a session waits for is one field on its own record (`AgentSession.waitingOn`)**, not a new collection: a session waits for one thing at a time (a second waiting call while one is held is refused; a new wait replaces one that only stands), and the windows, the foreman, the sleeper and routines all read that field. It survives a restart of the office with `held: false`. `wait_for_colleague` with no arguments continues it — a call that was cut never told the model the id of the request it had sent.
- **Shown as the existing WAITING state with a caption**, "Waiting for Sarah (T-12)". Rejected: a new activity state — every window already treats WAITING as "not working, not asking the owner", which is exactly this; a new state would change each of those lists for the same picture.
- **Refused before anything is sent**: for oneself, in a circle, for the foreman (and by the foreman: it works in rounds), for someone archived, paused, on a machine that is away or with a session that has no office tools, and across the line between the shared office and a member's own computer. A task one may not see is answered with the same words as one that does not exist. A circle is looked for among calls that are held right now only: a deadlock needs everyone in it blocked at the same moment, and whoever asks second is the one refused.
- **Cut short rather than left to the bound** when the awaited colleague is paused or archived, their session stops, their machine stays away or the task becomes nobody's. A session or machine that disappears is first given a moment (`timings.waitGoneGraceMs`, five seconds): sessions are stopped and started to move folders, links drop and come back, and the office going down looks the same for an instant. A wait that was cut short does not stand; the request stays open and its answer, if one comes, is a message.
- **A request someone waits for starts the colleague's session**, also when the owner never started them — as a routine's run does. An ordinary request still waits until they are there.
- **The foreman:** someone with a wait on record is never "stalled" or "idle". A `WAITING` item appears only when the awaited colleague has no session or sits between turns with nothing going on, after the wait and that idleness have both lasted two minutes (`timings.foremanWaitingMs`), once per wait. Rejected: no rule (the foreman would tell someone to "carry on" who cannot), and a rule on the length of the wait alone (a colleague who is at work on it is nobody's problem).
- **While it waits a session is not put to sleep, gets no routine run and no "you stopped" nudge** — also while the wait only stands. There is no time limit on that; the precedent is an open question to the owner, which does the same.

## 2026-11-10 — The hi-fi plays for the whole office
- **What is playing is the office's state; the sound is not.** The server keeps which station is on, since when and who switched it, tells every window (`{ t: 'radio' }`, also when one walks in), and each window plays the stream itself with its own audio element. Rejected: the server relaying audio — it would carry every listener's stream through the office's connection, need transcoding or buffering to keep people together, and add nothing a radio does not already do. The price: two people are not in sync to the second (each tuned in at its own moment), which for background radio nobody can hear unless they sit in the same real room.
- **In memory only.** After a restart of the office the hi-fi is off; nothing starts playing in people's windows because a server came back. The office does note which station was last played (`lastStationId`, as before).
- **Owners and colleagues switch it; visitors listen.** Switching is `work` on the shared office (the same line as the door bell and everything else colleagues do). The list of stations stays in the settings, so adding or removing one stays an owner's act: a station's address is loaded by every window in the office, so a play request names a saved station by id and can never carry an address of its own. Rejected: letting visitors switch (a 12-hour browser visit should not be able to change what everyone hears), and owners-only switching (the hi-fi is the office's, like the break room).
- **Loudness = my volume × distance.** Full within 3 m of the hi-fi, then a smooth fall (a smoothstep over a bent distance: no step, no kink, most of the fall in the first third) to a floor of 14% from 36 m on — the far corners of the standard office. A floor rather than silence because music for the whole office was asked for; a fixed reach rather than one scaled to the building, so adding member rooms does not make the hi-fi louder everywhere. Walls do not count in this version. The three numbers are constants in `packages/office-3d/src/hifi-sound.ts`.
- **The listener is the character, in every camera mode.** In the overview, free camera, command center and at someone's terminal the character stays where it was left, and that is where the music is heard from. Rejected: the camera's point of interest — flying the camera over the office would make the volume swing while the person, as everyone else sees them, has not moved.
- **Volume and mute are each computer's** (`prefs.json` / the page's storage), never the office's. Mute stops this window's download of the stream altogether instead of playing it at zero. The 3D hi-fi keeps moving in a muted window: it shows what the office is doing.
- **The element's own volume, eased twenty times a second** (a fifth of the way each step). Rejected: Web Audio gain nodes — routing a cross-origin stream through Web Audio needs the station to send CORS headers, and most do not (the output would be silence).
- **A refused autoplay is a notice, not an error.** In a browser that wants a click first, one calm line offers "click to listen"; the click is the gesture the browser wants.

## 2026-11-11 — People are sculpted to be read by their face
- **Stylised, not realistic and not toy blocks.** The skeleton, its bone lengths and every pose are unchanged (the chair, the desk and the gait depend on them); what changed is the envelope. The head is drawn 1.14× life size and the eyes are large (40 mm wide in a 206 mm head, the iris filling the opening from lid to lid): a person is mostly seen 60–130 pixels tall, and at that size a face is its eyes. A first attempt with life-sized eyes and a visible white all round looked uncanny close up and vanished from above. Rejected: realism (it needs textures and skinning this code does not have, and reads worse small); keeping the dark oval eyes (they read well small, and that was all).
- **One surface for the face.** Nose, lips, chin and the hollows of the eyes are displacements of the head's own surface on a grid that is fine across the face and coarse round the back; the rows of the grid bend with the mouth so the line between the lips runs along one of them. Lips, that line, a little warmth in the cheeks and the soft shadows under the nose and jaw are **vertex colours** multiplied with the skin colour. Rejected: separate nose, mouth and blush meshes lying on the head (more draw calls, and layers a fraction of a millimetre apart are what flickers).
- **Eyes are painted geometry too** (white, iris with a darker rim, pupil, catch-light: one mesh per iris colour, shared), with a wet material; the upper lids are two small meshes that turn about each eye's own axis. Rejected: one lid mesh for both eyes (the eyes are turned outward with the face, so one axis cannot close both), morph targets (a lid moving in a straight line cuts through the eye half-way).
- **Hair ends in the skin.** Every shell keeps a little thickness to its edge and then turns straight down into the head, so the hairline is the line where two surfaces cross, never two surfaces lying on one another; long hair is one surface down the outside, round the ends and back up the inside. Curls are domes in the shape of one shell (plus a soft bump map), not hundreds of balls: 4 000 triangles instead of 11 700, and no faceting.
- **Cloth is told apart by cut first, material second.** Each garment has its own collar, hem and cuffs; then a weave (the furniture's own height map), a knit drawn for the sweater, crisp plain cloth for the shirt, the stripe for the suit. Bump maps use a second set of coordinates laid on in metres (`clothUV`), because a suit's stripes need the first. Skin has no bump and mirrors little of the room.
- **Variety follows from the look.** A second hash of the same look picks the face, brows, eye colour (lighter eyes come with lighter skin), parting side, head width and girth. Nothing is stored and nothing was added to `Appearance`; the first hash still picks outfit, tie and shoes, so nobody changes clothes with this change. The owner's character has the face its fixed look gives it.
- **Budget.** A person may cost what one did: at most 30 000 triangles for the heaviest combination and 25 000 on average (`character.test.ts`), draw calls as before, no per-frame allocation, no post-processing. Every part goes through `finish()` (corners shared between triangles again after fusing, painting and laying cloth coordinates gave each triangle its own): without it the new figure cost a fifth more per person with 120 of them in view; with it, less than the old one. Eyes, lids and brows declare one common size so the office's distance tiers (People.tsx) show and hide them together.
- **A capture tool, not a preview UI.** `scripts/character-sheet.cjs` and `window.__orchScene.lineup` exist for looking at people while working on them; nothing is drawn unless a script sets it.

## 2026-11-10 — Member rooms (UPDATE plan, phase 4)
- **Derived on each client, never stored.** The rooms follow from the stored layout and from which personal hosts are online; a pure function gives every client the same answer. Rejected: writing rooms into the layout when someone connects — the layout is the owner's, and a crash would leave rooms behind.
- **Outside the outer wall, growing away from the building.** A room has a fixed width along the wall and gets deeper with more desks, so neither the office nor the neighbouring rooms move when a member hires. Rejected: growing along the wall (every later room would shift).
- **West wall first, then north, then east; never the entrance wall.** The brief said "the wall with the fewest things on it"; by that count the east wall wins in the default office, but the command centre's panel covers the east side of the picture, and the rooms must be seen from the overhead cameras. Doors are cut only where nothing hangs or stands, and a door that opens into a hall or the open floor is preferred to one into a room; the CEO's office is the last resort.
- **A place belongs to the n-th connected member**, not to a person: when someone leaves, those who enrolled after them move up one place. Rejected: remembering places per member on the client (clients would disagree about where a room is, and other people's characters are shown at shared coordinates).
- **At most 12 desks in a room.** A member with more personal employees sees the first twelve in the room and the number on the plaque; the rest are listed in the Team panel only.
- **Cameras are not changed for the rooms.** A room three pods deep on the west side reaches beyond the command centre's picture; the camera staying put mattered more (the owner's standing preference for a calm camera).

## 2026-11-10 — Members, roles and personal hosts (UPDATE plan)
- **An invitation is a short link; the credential is made when it is used.** (2026-11-11; before, the invitation was the credential: one long text with the addresses, the fingerprint and the token.) The owner asked for inviting to be much simpler than sending "one huge string". Now the owner sends `https://office.example.com/join/K7QF-M2XP-9TRD`; the app exchanges the code once at the door for the same connection it kept before. Still no passwords or SSO. What this buys besides length: a link works once and for a week, so one found later in a chat history opens nothing; and nothing secret is on screen for longer than it takes to copy. Rejected: a link that carries the token (as long as before, and good forever); a custom `orchestrator://` link the browser hands to the app (needs the app registered on three systems; pasting works everywhere); letting the link sign a person in from a browser (a browser is a window to look through, never a seat — see the door bell).

- **Colleagues may approve permission requests of shared employees.** "Works on all shared projects" includes the terminal, where an approval can be typed anyway. Approvals for someone else's personal employee stay with that person and with owners.
- **The root owner token never crosses the network.** Remote owners are members with the role `owner`. The first one is invited from a terminal on the server (`pnpm office invite`).
- **"Shared" and "personal" are a property of the host.** An employee, session or project is personal exactly when its host belongs to a member. No per-object ownership field to keep in sync.
- **A personal host is a normal host agent** started by the desktop app, enrolled through an endpoint only its member can call, and reached through a relay on the member's own loopback that pins the office's certificate. Rejected: teaching the agent and Claude Code's MCP client to trust a self-signed certificate.
- **A member's room exists while their host is online.** Closing the app stops the agent; the tmux sessions stay and are adopted again next time.

## 2026-11-10 — Routines (the owner: "check it every five minutes and put what is new on the task board")
- A routine is nothing but a message the office repeats: it goes into the employee's real session by the same path as the owner's own words (`Comms.sendMessage`), so it is in the conversation, the feed and the record, and the employee acts on it with the tools they already have. No second execution path, no headless runs.
- **Skipped when busy, never queued behind.** Mid-turn, a question or approval open, or the run before not yet delivered: this run does not happen, and the routine is looked at again one interval later. A check that repeats is worthless late and harmful in a burst. The next run is counted from the look, not from the slots that were missed, so an office that was down for a day runs each routine once.
- **Held, not counted down,** while the employee or the project is paused, outside working hours, or the machine is away: it runs once when that is over. (Outcome `not_run` / `skipped_offline`; this is one outcome more than the four first planned, because "paused" is not "busy".)
- Two routines of one employee that come due together are both handed over, one after the other: only *other* things waiting count as busy, or the later one would never run.
- No session: the routine starts it, as talking to them does — also for someone who was never started or whom the owner stopped. To stop a routine, pause or remove the routine.
- Default every 5 minutes, minimum 1, maximum a day, at most 10 per employee. One minute is allowed because the owner asked for minutes; the cost is said where the choice is made.
- **Found items become proposed tasks, once.** The existing `propose_tasks` tool got a `dedupe_key`; no new tool. The key is compared against every task the project has, done ones included: the same ticket is still on the support desk long after its task is finished. Tasks with a key are never released automatically, even where the project assigns work by itself: a check that misreads a page must not start work on its own. (The owner may want these released automatically in some projects; that would be a project policy, not built.)
- No permission is widened for routines. A run that needs a tool the employee must ask for asks, every time, until the owner allows that tool in the project's policy.

## 2026-11-10 — Sleep and wake (the owner: "sessions should shut down to save memory, and continue from where they stopped")
- Sleep is the ordinary clean stop (`session.kill`: the tmux session and the process end), marked `asleepSince` on the session record. Not a suspended process: a stopped one gives all its memory back, and Claude Code keeps the conversation on disk either way.
- **Only when nothing can be lost**: between turns (known from hooks — a session without hooks is never put to sleep), no task in hand, no question or approval open, nothing queued or in flight to it, nobody at its terminal, not paused by the owner, not in a meeting, the foreman not in a round. "Blocked" and "waiting for input with no task" may sleep: what unblocks them wakes them.
- **Default 30 minutes, on.** The owner asked for it; half an hour is long enough that someone in a back-and-forth with the owner is not stopped between two messages, and short enough to matter on a server with many employees. (Not measured: what a resume costs in tokens compared with a session that stayed up.) 0 switches it off; the smallest other value is 5, because a start costs seconds and a resume re-reads the conversation.
- **Shared machines only** in this version. The owner said "on the server"; what runs on a member's own computer uses that member's memory and account and is theirs to keep running. A per-member switch can come later.
- **Who is woken for what.** Someone the office put to sleep is started again for *anything* that is queued for them (message, answer, meeting, report request, foreman's instruction, task). Someone who was never started, crashed or was stopped by the owner is started only by what always did that (a task, talking to them from the Talk window) and by a routine. So sleep is invisible, and an explicit stop still holds.
- Waking happens in the delivery queue (`Comms.pump`): whatever path queues something needs no knowledge of sleep. One attempt at a time per employee; a failed one is not repeated for 30 s; a machine that is away is waited for.
- A woken session starts in the folder it was in (the task in hand's folder, else the last one if it is still theirs), so `--resume` finds its conversation; the office does not touch branches on a wake.
- A start that asked for a particular folder while another start of the same employee was in flight used to get that other start's session, wherever it was. It now looks again afterwards (the session is moved if needed): a message and a task arriving together must not put the task in the wrong folder.
- A resumed session that ends before its first hook could not continue its conversation: the feed says so, and the next start begins a new one instead of trying the same resume for ever.

## A member's own computer on Windows: WSL2, started by the app (2026-11-11)
A collaborator on Windows could use everything shared and had no room: sessions need tmux.

- **The app stays on Windows; the host runs in WSL2.** Running the whole app inside WSL was tried first and is not usable: Electron there gets no graphics card (the GPU process fails and the office is drawn on the CPU). So the window is a Windows program and only what needs tmux is in WSL: the relay, the enrolment and the host agent — the very code that runs on macOS and Linux (`personal-host.cjs`), as a process of its own (`personal-host-cli.cjs`).
- **The relay runs in WSL too, not in the app.** WSL2 has its own network: a program inside does not reach `127.0.0.1` of Windows, where the app's relay would listen. Inside WSL the agent, Claude Code's MCP client and the relay are on one loopback, as on a Mac.
- **Nothing to install in WSL for the office.** The three files the host consists of are written into `~/.orchestrator/desktop-host/` on every start, from the app's own copy. That also works for a packaged app, whose files WSL cannot read, and the host can never be older than the app.
- **One `wsl.exe`, a script on its standard input, no command line.** Quoting through `wsl.exe` differs between versions; a script on stdin needs none. The script finds a Node.js that is new enough (the one on PATH is often the distribution's old one: `~/.local/node22`, nvm, volta and `ORCH_NODE` are tried) and replaces itself with the host, which reads the rest of the same input: the office and **the member's token, which is therefore never on a command line**. Closing that pipe is how the host is told to stop — it also closes when the app dies.
- **Folders: the WSL home, the Windows profile, and what the person names.** On a Mac the allowed root is the home folder, which is where a person's code is. On Windows the code is usually on a Windows drive, so the profile folder (`/mnt/c/Users/<name>`) is allowed as the home's counterpart; other drives are **not** opened by default — owners can reach into a member's computer, and "every drive" is more than a home folder. `ORCH_PERSONAL_ROOTS` names more (`D:\Work`). Rejected: all of `/mnt`.
- **Sessions run in WSL, so they see Linux**: Linux tools, the WSL Claude login, Windows files through `/mnt` (slower than WSL's own disk). A project that must run with Windows tools is not served by this.

## Deleting a project (2026-11-11)
The owner asked to be able to delete projects, as an owner. Until then a project could only be paused; `archived` exists as a status but nothing in the app sets it.

- **Work in progress is interrupted first, in the real terminal** (the same as pausing), so nobody goes on working for a project that is gone. A project one of whose tasks is being verified at that moment is refused, with the task's name: the verification would write its result into a task that no longer exists.
- **Nothing is left pointing at it.** Open questions about it are withdrawn; tasks of other projects no longer depend on its tasks; other projects no longer depend on it; lessons learned for this project alone are retired and suggestions about it dismissed (company and employee lessons stay); meetings and reports stay as a record without a project. Events and outcomes keep its id as history.
- **What was deleted stays deleted** even if a test run or merge that started before the delete finishes after it: the store drops a write for a project or task that was removed (`Core.put`).

## The task board in the employees' hands (2026-11-11)
The owner asked that any agent can manage tasks — release them, bring them back from blocked and from review — and start other agents, "the system should be as autonomous as possible".

- **On by default, one switch to take it back** (Settings → Employees). The owner asked for it for this office; another office, or this one on another day, may want the board to itself, and a setting is cheaper than a regret.
- **Any employee, not a role.** A "manager" role was rejected: the point is that whoever sees what is needed does it. The foreman keeps its own tools (it supervises everyone and its actions are counted on its rounds) and gains release, unblock and wake.
- **What stays the owner's:** permission requests (unchanged: no agent, the foreman included, decides one); marking work DONE without verification; deleting tasks and projects; a project that is paused; a task the owner blocked by hand; project policy. "As autonomous as possible" was read as "work does not stop for want of a board move", not "nothing is the owner's any more".
- **Reviews: anyone but the author.** With the board in their hands, a teammate who was not appointed may review work that waits. The author may not — that is the one thing that makes it a review.
- **Unblocking needs the how.** `unblock_task` refuses an empty note: the next person reads the note instead of the reasoning that is gone with the caller's context.
- **Waking carries a message.** A session started with nothing to read would sit idle and be put back to sleep; so `wake_colleague` requires what the colleague is needed for and delivers it first. An earlier open question — should a waited request start someone the owner stopped — is answered by this request: yes, agents may start colleagues; only someone the owner *paused* stays as they are.
- **Risk accepted and written down:** a repeating check that proposes tasks (`dedupe_key`) used to wait for the owner to release each one; an employee can now release them. The record says who did.

## The vault (2026-11-11)
The owner asked for one place on the server where all credentials are kept: employees recognise passwords and keys and put them there, other employees use them when they connect to something, and the vault stands in the office as a thing you can walk up to. The parsing was to follow the owner's Ahoy project.

- **Names travel, values do not.** Taken from Ahoy: a credential gets a short name, and everything that is written down or passed on carries `{{vault:name}}`. A message with a password in it is delivered with the name in its place.
- **Used through a command, not a tool that returns the value.** Ahoy fills the value into a tool's arguments at the last moment. Here the tools are Claude Code's own, and rewriting a command through a hook would have to come with a permission decision — it would let a command through that the owner had not approved. So each session gets a small helper, and a command asks for the value where it needs it: `$("$ORCH_VAULT" get name)`. The model sees the name, the shell gets the value, and the permission prompt shows the command as written. Rejected: a `vault_get` tool (every credential would end up in conversations, transcripts and on terminals visitors can watch).
- **What is caught by itself is only what cannot be mistaken, or what the sentence says outright.** Ahoy asks a small model to judge the rest ("is this word a password?"); the office's server has no model of its own. The reader is one: employees are told to store what is plainly a credential. So code like `token = process.env.TOKEN` is never taken for a secret, at the cost of missing a bare password with nothing around it.
- **One vault, everybody's.** No per-project vaults and no per-credential permissions: "any agent can use anything" was the request. People: owners manage and may look at values; colleagues see the names and add; visitors nothing.
- **The key is a file beside the database.** Encryption protects a copied database and a leaked backup of it without the key; it does not protect against someone who can read the server's data folder. A passphrase typed at every start was rejected: the office has to come back by itself after a reboot.
- **Deleting is real**, and says so: the value is gone from here; whatever it opened still opens until it is changed there.

## The long memory is MemPalace, behind two tools of the office's own (2026-11-11)
The owner chose way A of `plans/IDEAS-memory-from-mempalace.md`: install MemPalace on the server and give it to the employees.

- **MemPalace itself, not a copy of its ideas** — the owner's decision. It finds by meaning and across languages (the owner writes Serbian, the records are mostly English), which a word search of our own would not.
- **Behind `recall` and `remember`, not as a second MCP server in every session.** MemPalace has an MCP server of its own with some 47 tools. Giving that to every employee would put 47 tool descriptions into every session, start a Python process with the embedding model per session on a shared server, and leave members' own computers without it. Instead the office's server talks to MemPalace and employees talk to the office: two tools, one palace, every machine, the office's own rules about what is whose, and the vault in front.
- **Through its command line, one call at a time.** Each call loads the model and lets it go: about 4.5 s for a search, nothing resident. The owner's server is shared and has frozen under memory pressure before (Ahoy's notes); a daemon holding the model was left for when the wait is felt.
- **The office files; the employee asks.** What is worth remembering is filed without anybody deciding to: finished tasks, answers, resolved blockers, lessons, decisions. `remember` is for what none of those captures. A task is handed over with what is close to its subject, because an employee who does not know there is something to ask does not ask.
- **Fail open.** A slow or broken memory costs a task nothing: the hand-over waits twelve seconds and goes on without.
- **Not filed:** anything from a member's own computer; permission requests; terminal output. Records go through the redactor, and notes through the vault's capture.
- **The multilingual model**, 300 MB against 30: tried both on the server — the small one scored a Serbian question at 0.03 against the English record it should find; the multilingual one at 0.60.

## A shared folder is a queue, not a blocker (2026-11-11)
_Since the same day the queue is a project's choice (`policy.oneAtATime`), not the default: see "A shared folder is worked in side by side" at the top._

The owner found many employees standing with a red mark: "T-18 is blocked: … is already changing files in /home/…/xagio (T-3). Two employees must not edit the same working tree". The rule is right — two sessions must not change one folder at once — but reporting it as a blocked task was wrong: nothing was wrong with the task, nobody could do anything about it except wait, and nothing unblocked it when the folder became free.

- **Waiting is not being blocked.** The task stays READY with a note that says whom it waits for; no notification, no red mark. The scheduler tries it again whenever something changes, and hands it over when the other task is no longer in progress.
- **One at a time per folder, in the order the scheduler already uses** (priority, then age). No separate queue was built.
- **The real cure is isolation**: a project on a git repository with worktrees gives every employee a folder of their own and nobody waits. That is said in the project's settings, not shouted at the owner per task.
- Left as it is: a task that is in progress but whose employee has stopped without completing it still holds the folder. That is "someone stopped working", which the foreman and the *stalled* notice already cover.

## Voices go through the office, not between the windows (2026-11-11)
The owner asked for push-to-talk whose loudness follows the distance between characters and which does not leave the conference room.

- **Relayed by the office over the WebSocket it already has, not WebRTC.** Peer-to-peer would need STUN/TURN and signalling, and members sit behind the TLS door, Tailscale and a reverse proxy; the office already has an authenticated socket to every window and already knows where every character stands. Cost: a little more delay and the office's bandwidth (about 22 kB/s per speaker per listener). For the handful of people an office has, that is the simpler thing that works everywhere the office works.
- **Who hears is decided by the office, how loud by the window.** The office drops a frame for anyone out of range (26 m) or on the other side of a closed meeting room's wall, so a window cannot listen in by ignoring the rule. The window plays what arrives at a loudness from the distance (`voiceFalloff`, silent from 22 m — inside the office's range, so a voice fades and is never cut).
- **A meeting room is sound-proof only while it is a room**: `kind: 'meeting'` and closed walls. Member rooms are not: a member's closed room is about who may act on their employees, not about sound.
- **Telephone quality, no codec library**: 16 kHz mono, µ-law, 512 samples a frame, base64 in JSON. Opus would be a third of the size but needs WebCodecs or a WASM build; not worth it yet.
- **Nothing is stored.** Frames are passed on and forgotten; there is no recording and no history of who spoke.
- **Push-to-talk only.** No open microphone, so nobody is on the air without holding a key; the microphone is released a minute after the last use. *(Changed the same day: see the next entry.)*

## An open microphone is a choice, behind a gate and a light (2026-11-11)
The collaborator asked, after the first real use of push-to-talk, to be able to talk without holding a key, and to send the office's sound to headphones.

- **Push-to-talk stays the default.** *Microphone always on* is a preference of this computer (`voiceMode`), never the office's: nobody is put on the air by somebody else's setting, and a new computer starts with the key.
- **A gate, not a stream.** An open microphone sends only frames louder than a mark the person sets (root mean square; 0.02 unless changed), stays open about 0.45 s after the last loud frame so the gaps between words are not cut, and sends the frame before the first loud one so a word's beginning is not clipped (`voice-gate.ts`). Without it every open microphone would send 21 kB/s of room noise to everyone in earshot and the green dot beside a name would mean nothing. The gate is in the window; the office's rules (range, the meeting room, 45 frames a second) are unchanged and need no knowledge of it.
- **The talk key becomes the switch.** With the microphone always on, V switches it off and on (one key to remember, and the one already under the finger). Switched off, the microphone is let go of after a minute, as with the key, so the system's own microphone light goes out.
- **It is always shown.** While the microphone is always on the pill at the bottom of the window never goes away: on, talking, or off. Push-to-talk shows it only while the key is held, as before.
- **It keeps going when the window is not in front.** Losing the keyboard ends push-to-talk (the key's release would not arrive); an open microphone is meant for talking while doing something else, so it goes on — which is what the light and the switch are for.
- **One output device for everything the office plays.** Voices, the hi-fi and the effects all go where *Sound comes out of* says (`sound-devices.ts`: `AudioContext.setSinkId` and the audio element's `setSinkId`), rather than one choice per kind of sound. Where the browser cannot do that (Firefox, Safari) the list is disabled and says to use the system's settings. Device ids are the browser's own, per computer and per page origin, so they are kept with this computer's preferences and mean nothing anywhere else.
